## What the program is based on

Each source opens on the study's page: there you will find the abstract and a link to the full text.

| Component                                           | Source                                                                                                                                     | What it showed                                                                                                                                       |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Strength training to a noticeable effort            | [ACSM Position Stand, 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19516148/), [Fiatarone, 1994, NEJM](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8190152/)  | Muscles grow even at 90, but only with loads of 70–85% of maximum; light exercise does not restore muscle mass                                       |
| High-intensity strength training and impact loading | [LIFTMOR, Watson, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28975661/)                                                                         | In women with low bone density, 8 months of heavy squats and deadlifts increased bone mineral density in the spine and femoral neck with no injuries |
| Balance from the Otago program                      | [Campbell, 1997, BMJ](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9366737/), [Sherrington, 2019, Cochrane](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30703272/)  | About a quarter fewer falls; the effect grows with a volume of 3 or more hours per week                                                              |
| Power training (movement speed)                     | [Reid and Fielding, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22016147/), [Sayers and Gibson, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22500229/) | Leg power declines faster than strength and predicts falls better; it is trained with fast repetitions and moderate weight                           |
| Back extensors                                      | [Sinaki, 2002, Bone](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12052450/)                                                                            | Two years of back extension exercises led to 2.7 times fewer compression fractures 10 years later                                                    |
| Dual tasks                                          | [Dual-task meta-analysis, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38364709/)                                                                 | Doing a mental and a physical task at the same time improves balance                                                                                 |
| Total volume                                        | [WHO guidelines, 2020](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240015128)                                                              | 150–300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity, strength training on 2+ days, multicomponent balance training on 3+ days a week                        |
| Protein                                             | [PROT-AGE, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23867520/), [ESPEN, 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24814383/)                      | 1.0–1.2 g of protein per kg of body weight per day, 25–30 g per meal; otherwise strength training does not build muscle                              |
| Home test norms                                     | [Rikli and Jones, 2013, Senior Fitness Test](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22613940/)                                                    | The “below normal” thresholds in the tests, given separately for each age band                                                                       |

## What age it is for

The program is written for women aged 60 and over. What matters is not your age on paper but your condition: postmenopause, declining bone density, loss of muscle mass and movement speed. The studies it is built on enrolled participants well under 70.

| Program block                         | Age of study participants                                  |
| ------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Strength and impact loading (LIFTMOR) | 65 ± 5 years                                               |
| Back module (Sinaki)                  | 49–65 years at the start, 10-year follow-up                |
| Fall prevention (Cochrane)            | 60 and over; the benefit in the 75+ group was no different |
| Otago balance (Campbell)              | 80 and over, mean age 84                                   |

**If you are 60–69.** Move through the phases faster: learning the technique usually takes two weeks instead of four. Keep your effort in the upper part of the range and add working weight more boldly. You can cut the Tuesday balance block in half and give the time you free up to the strength part and to hops.

**If you are 80 or older, or you have had falls.** The opposite: do the full balance block in every session, slow down the weight progression, and do single-leg exercises strictly next to a support. Replace hops with heel drops.

## Before you start

1. See your primary care doctor or a cardiologist: blood pressure, ECG, blood tests including vitamin D. Tell your doctor that you plan to do strength training to a noticeable effort.
2. Bone density scan (DXA). It is recommended for all women aged 65 and older in any case ([USPSTF](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/osteoporosis-screening)). If osteoporosis is found, the program stays, but we remove exercises that bend the spine under load and jumps, and keep the heel drops.
3. Do the home tests and write down your results. This is your starting point.
4. Prepare your space: 2 × 2 m of clear floor, a chair without wheels against a wall, a non-slip mat, shoes with non-slip soles.

## Disclaimer

The program is for educational purposes and does not replace medical advice. Get a checkup before you start, and stop if you notice warning symptoms.

I put it together for my mother, an active woman with no serious limitations. The program is based on the published studies listed above and is not a medical prescription: it does not take into account your health condition, past injuries or the medicines you take.
